# Business Evaluation Principles — Kai Hamil Guiding Doc

**Source:** Quartz Leadership newsletter, June 2026
**Status:** Active reference for business evaluation, product/offer design, pricing, and operational decisions across Kai Hamil ventures (Valé, Groove Pal, Time & Focus, Couples Valé, Fieldhouse, Workshop, etc.)

> The same product can feel like a steal or a rip-off depending on how it's framed, priced, and guaranteed.

---

## 1. Offer Supersedes Product (Alex Hormozi — "Grand Slam Offer")

People will actively choose a more expensive option when the framing, bonuses, and guarantees wrapping the product make it feel like the only rational choice.

**For Kai Hamil:**
- Always lead with **outcome → bonuses → guarantee → price**, not price → product → maybe outcome.
- When pricing a workshop, Valé OS, or any KH offering, the price line goes LAST.
- Build a real guarantee into every offer (money back, results-based, white-glove support, etc.).
- The product is rarely the bottleneck. The offer almost always is.

**Apply to:**
- Workshop landing pages
- Valé OS sales pages
- Fieldhouse membership pitch
- Groove Pal joining tier

---

## 2. Solve for Failure First (Charlie Munger — Inversion Thinking)

> "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." — Charlie Munger

Failure is easier to predict than success. Before asking what would make a plan work, ask what would destroy it.

**For Kai Hamil:**
- Before launching any new venture, run an explicit pre-mortem: "What would guarantee this fails?"
- Fix the top two risks **before** allocating budget or time.
- Already implicitly used in product rubrics (kill conditions for Groove Pal, Time & Focus).
- Make this an explicit step in any new spec doc.

**Apply to:**
- New product launches (Phase-gate)
- Workshop cohort design
- Course curriculum (where does a student drop out?)
- HOA / client engagements

---

## 3. Psycho-Logic (Rory Sutherland — London-to-Paris Train Example)

Engineers wanted billions to make the train faster. Sutherland: hire sommeliers and chefs so passengers wish the ride were longer.

**People buy meaning and status, not raw specs.**

**For Kai Hamil:**
- Before improving a product, ask: can I improve the **framing, name, setting, or story** for free first?
- This is already part of the KH DNA — "system development practice not holding company" was a reframe, not a rebuild.
- Valé OS isn't "an automation system" — it's "a system of agentic intelligence around your most valuable hours."
- Groove Pal isn't "vinyl sharing" — it's "a pen pal network where vinyl is the letter."

**Apply to:**
- Product naming
- Landing page copy
- Workshop positioning
- Substack essay angles

---

## 4. Profit First (Mike Michalowicz)

Most owners treat profit as leftovers — and there are rarely leftovers.

Skim profit into a separate account the **moment money arrives**, then run the business on what remains.

**For Kai Hamil:**
- When KH workshops or Valé OS sales start hitting accounts, set up a dedicated profit account immediately.
- Route a fixed % off the top before any operating expense touches it.
- Treat profit as the FIRST line item, not the residual.
- This applies even at modest revenue ($5K/cohort) — the discipline forms early.

**Apply to:**
- Workshop revenue
- Valé OS sales
- Groove Pal subscription / sales
- HOA client invoices (Gatefield, future clients)

---

## 5. Blame the System (W. Edwards Deming)

When something goes wrong, look at the system before the person.

Before reacting to a bad week's numbers, plot the last several weeks. Ask: ordinary noise or genuine signal?

**For Kai Hamil:**
- Don't over-react to single data points (one workshop sign-up, one bad rating in Time & Focus, one slow week of engagement).
- Build dashboards that show trend lines, not point-in-time numbers.
- The Time & Focus app's weekly/monthly/quarterly views ARE this principle in product form.
- When something fails (the Apr 21 morning brief, the 7am cron job), examine the system, not "did I mess up." Almost always the system.

**Apply to:**
- Brief / publication system reliability
- Workshop conversion rates
- Customer churn (when KH has customers)
- Personal productivity (Time & Focus app metrics)

## Synthesis — How These Compose

These aren't five separate rules. They reinforce each other:

| Principle | When to Use |
|-----------|-------------|
| Offer Supersedes | Designing how to sell anything |
| Solve for Failure | Designing what to build |
| Psycho-Logic | Designing how it feels |
| Profit First | Designing how it sustains |
| Blame the System | Designing how to learn |

**The flow:** Solve for Failure → Build → Frame with Psycho-Logic → Sell with Offer → Capture with Profit First → Learn with System Thinking.

## How Stitch Should Use This

When evaluating a Kai Hamil business decision (new product, pricing change, launch plan, postmortem):

1. **Run an inversion pass.** What would make this fail?
2. **Check the offer structure.** Is price last? Is there a real guarantee?
3. **Audit the framing.** Could a cheaper, faster reframe deliver more than the planned investment?
4. **Confirm profit allocation.** Is profit a first expense or a leftover?
5. **Don't over-react to single signals.** Plot the trend before changing course.

Reference this doc explicitly in any business evaluation output:
> "Per the business evaluation principles in `learn/business-evaluation-principles.md`..."

# On Being Orbited

*The weight you carry in any room is a dial*

---

Here is the whole idea in one sentence, so you can decide right now whether to keep reading: the weight you carry in any room is not a fixed trait you were issued at birth, it is a dial, and most people never reach for it.

You've sat in that meeting. The one where you had the answer, said nothing, and watched a worse decision get made.

We call this personal gravity. Gravity, usefully, has one defining behavior — it pulls, it never pushes. The loudest person in the meeting is applying *force*. They shove decisions, they talk over the orbits of others, they win by pressure, and they have to do it again tomorrow because force has to be reapplied constantly and quietly breeds resistance. The person with gravity does something else entirely. They bend the field so that attention and decisions drift toward them on their own. Nobody resents being pulled the way they resent being pushed. This is why the heaviest person in an organization is so rarely the loudest one.

I'm going to assume you opened this for one of two reasons, because there are really only two.

You're tired of watching your ideas leave the room attached to someone else's name. You are doing good work that dissolves the moment it leaves your hands, and you are tired of being the most competent invisible person in the building.

Or you've started to dread your own calendar because every meeting needs you there or it falls apart. You have somehow become the center everything orbits, every decision routes through you, and you have started to mistake "everything depends on me" for importance when it is actually just a bottleneck wearing a nice coat.

Both of those are gravity problems. Same dial, turned different directions. The good news is that it is, in fact, a dial.

## The three things the dial actually controls

There isn't one knob, there are three, and almost everyone only knows about the first.

**Mass.** This is the slow, dense, real kind — a track record, a domain only you understand, the resources or information others need to get through their day. You build it over years and it doesn't evaporate. It's also the one you can do the least about this afternoon, which is why fixating on it alone is a trap.

**Direction.** Gravity, left alone, is diffuse. It pulls equally on everything nearby, which means a high-mass person with no aim leaks influence in every direction and moves nothing in particular. Concentration is the skill: pointing your pull at the two or three things that genuinely need to move and letting everything else fall outside your field. A person of modest mass who aims precisely will out-influence a heavier person who sprays it everywhere. This is the difference between being "really smart" and being the person people call when *this specific thing* is on fire.

**Amplitude** — including turning it down. The dial nobody touches. A high-gravity person who never withholds becomes a black hole: nothing nearby gets its own orbit, nothing can form without them, and the system quietly becomes a dependency. Knowing how to lower your gravity, on purpose, is not modesty. It is engineering. This is knowing when to skip the meeting so someone else can own it.

## What this looks like on a Tuesday

The framework is only worth anything if you can see yourself in it, so here are the moves in the form you'll actually meet them.

**You did the work and nobody knows.** The meeting ends. The decision gets made. You had the better answer and you watched it not get spoken. Stop solving the visibility problem by working harder — more mass aimed nowhere just makes you a denser invisible object. Aim it instead. Attach your name to the artifact at the moment it's most useful to others, not after. Be the one who frames the problem in the room before anyone frames the solution, because the person who defines the question is the well everything orbits. You are not asking for credit; you are placing your mass where the field can find it.

**You've become the bottleneck.** Turn it down, deliberately. Find the decision that doesn't actually need you and visibly route it elsewhere. Create a gravity-free zone — a meeting you don't attend, a call you don't make — so another well has room to form. This feels like losing importance. It is the opposite. A thing that only works while you stand in the middle of it isn't an asset, it's a liability with your name on it. Being the center is heavy. Anyone who has been there will tell you, usually quietly, that it is heavy to be the thing everyone leans toward.

**You want to grow someone.** This is the most generous use of the dial, and the least understood. You have accumulated mass; you can lend some of it before the other person has earned their own. You do it by visibly orbiting *them* — asking their read in front of the room, deferring publicly, hanging their name on the win. Everyone watching feels the borrowed pull as real, and it buys the person time for their own track record to catch up and make it permanent. This is the whole difference between mentoring and sponsoring. Mentoring is advice given in private. Sponsoring is gravity spent in public. One of them changes careers.

**Someone's gravity is distorting the field.** The brilliant colleague whose certainty keeps bending decisions the wrong way. You don't fight force with force. You reduce their pull by routing around them and seeding other wells, so the field rebalances. Done bluntly it's exile and everyone notices. Done well it just looks like the room got wiser.

## What if you don't have mass yet?

Maybe you read this and thought: *This assumes I have something to turn down. I don't. I'm new, or junior, or in a room where my track record hasn't formed yet.*

Fair. The dial still exists, but your first job is building the mass. Think of it as exercise and diet for gravity:

**Build a domain.** Become the person who knows something specific that others need. Not everything. One thing. The reporting tool nobody else understands. The customer segment only you talk to. The process only you documented. Domain is the foundation of mass.

**Ship visible artifacts.** Ideas dissolve. Documents, tools, and decisions persist. Every time you attach your name to something that outlasts the meeting, you add mass. A spreadsheet that becomes the source of truth. A checklist that prevents the recurring mistake. A decision memo that frames the choice.

**Accumulate small yeses.** Gravity builds through accumulated trust. Each time you deliver what you promised, you add mass. Each time you show up prepared, you add mass. Each time you make someone else look good, you add mass. This is slow. This is the only way.

Once you have mass, the other two dials become available. Until then, focus here.

## The conservation law

One honest caveat keeps this from curdling into a power fantasy: gravity is mutual. Mass attracts mass both ways. To be orbited is also to be pulled — the people you sponsor acquire a claim on you, the thing you built leans back on you, the field you bend bends back. So the goal was never to maximize your gravity. It was to spend it deliberately: concentrate it where it matters, lend it to grow others, and withhold it often enough that the world can stand up without you holding the middle.

## The part you keep

Here's what you actually walk away with, which is bigger than any single move above. You now have a lens. The next time you feel stuck, overlooked, overextended, or oddly load-bearing in a situation you didn't mean to carry, you can run one quiet diagnostic: *where is the gravity here, and is mine turned the right way?* Sometimes the answer is add mass. Sometimes it's aim it. Surprisingly often it's turn it down. The situation doesn't change — your reading of it does, and with it the one adjustment most likely to improve your state.

It was a dial the whole time. You know where it is. Reach for it.

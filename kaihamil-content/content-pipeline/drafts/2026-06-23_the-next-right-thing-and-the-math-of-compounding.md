# The Next Right Thing, and the Math Behind It

In *Frozen II*, Anna sits alone in the dark. Her sister is gone. The world she anchored to has come apart. She has no plan, no certainty, and no map.

What she has is a song. It tells her to do the next right thing.

The song landed hard for a reason. It named something most people feel constantly and can't quite say. It also, almost as an aside, turned out to be a theory of compounding. Take it apart slowly, word by word, and what you find under the surface is a piece of practical math.

Four words. Each one doing real work.

## "Do"

To act. Even when the action you choose is to stay still.

There is a difference between stillness as a choice and stillness as a collapse. From outside they can look the same. From inside they are completely different. One is a person waiting on purpose, gathering themselves, refusing to make the situation worse by moving badly. The other is a person frozen, paying interest on the anxiety of not moving.

The verb does not require motion. It requires *agency*. You can do stillness on purpose and that counts. You can do thinking on purpose and that counts. What you cannot do is replace the verb with worry and call that an action. Worry pretends to be doing something. It is not.

Action, even small action, is the antidote to the spiral. Worry is the spiral. Pick the verb.

## "Next"

Not the thing far away. Not the goal. Not the finish line. The next step. The one available to you now.

This is where the song's wisdom is easiest to miss, because the world keeps telling you to think bigger. The seven-run home run does not exist. Here is what does exist:

Imagine your team is down seven runs and you have seven batters coming up. The announcer says you need a seven-run home run. That isn't a thing. So let's walk what is.

The first batter singles. Bases: one runner on first. No runs scored.

The second batter singles. Runners on first and second. No runs scored.

The third batter singles. Bases loaded. Still no runs scored.

The fourth batter singles. The runner on third scores. One run. Bases still loaded.

The fifth batter singles. Another run scores. Two runs. Bases still loaded.

The sixth batter singles. Another run. Three runs. Bases loaded.

The seventh batter hits a grand slam. Four runs at once. Seven runs total.

Six singles followed by a home run scores the same seven runs that seven straight home runs would have scored. None of those seven batters got out. None of them did anything spectacular except the last one — and *the last one was only spectacular because of what the others did first.*

This is the lesson hiding inside "next." The early steps don't look like progress. They don't put runs on the board. They put runners on base. Most days, your work is loading the bases. The grand-slam moment, when it comes, will pay out against the bases you loaded — not against zero.

You probably cannot take the last step first. You can almost always take the next one.

## "Right"

Aligned with the good you are aiming at.

This presumes you have named your good. That's not hard. Good is the target you have already chosen — learn a skill, reduce debt, make friends, raise healthy children, earn more, get sober, finish the book. Whatever it is. *Right* is the move that points toward it, even a little. If you have not named your target yet, the first next right thing is to name it.

The negative version of right is the wrong thing — the move that points away. Smashing the lamp because you're angry at the bill. Quitting the job in a moment of frustration. Burning the friendship over the smaller version of the argument. The wrong thing isn't always the move that fails to solve your problem. Most moves won't solve your problem. The wrong thing is the move that makes it worse.

This narrows the definition of right beautifully. Right doesn't mean optimal. Right doesn't mean clever. Right doesn't mean solves the whole thing. Right means *aligned* — pointed toward, not away from, the good you've already chosen.

You can do the next right thing without solving anything. The right thing today might just be not breaking what's left.

## "Thing"

A real object. A concrete action you can name and watch yourself doing.

This is the word that closes the door on rumination. Verbs without objects are fantasies. *I'll work on it.* *I'll figure it out.* *I'll do something about it.* These sentences feel like action and are not action. They are sentences about action, taking up the space where action would go.

The thing has a shape. It has a time. It can be done badly or done well. You can finish it. Someone watching would be able to tell.

Without the thing, the verb has nothing to land on.

## Why this is also a theory of compounding

Here is the part the song doesn't say out loud.

Each next right thing leaves you in a slightly better position than the one you started in. You're a step closer. You're on a higher rung. The next next-right-thing, when it arrives, is chosen from that higher rung.

This is what compounding actually is. Compounding is not a finance concept. Compounding is what happens when growth lands on an existing base instead of starting over each time. Money is the most legible example because the math is easy to write down. But the same shape is happening everywhere.

You compound your ability to learn by first learning to read. Now every book is open to you.

You compound your ability to earn by first earning some. Now you have options the broke version of you didn't have.

You compound your ability to make friends by first making one. Now you have someone who can introduce you to others, vouch for you, model what trust feels like.

You compound your ability to write a book by first writing a paragraph. Then by writing one tomorrow on top of the one you wrote today.

The mechanism is the same in every case: *position-building actions, taken from successively higher positions.* The grand slam pays out four runs because three runners are already on. The fluent reader learns faster because they can read. The earner earns more because they have capital, networks, and the confidence that comes from having done it before.

This reframes "the next right thing" from a moral instruction into something far more practical: **it is the only available mechanism for engaging with a compounding system.** Big swings don't compound. They either work or they don't, and they don't leave a position behind for the next swing to start from. Compounding requires the next-right-thing posture. The two aren't related by analogy. They're related by mechanism.

## The hard part

Compounding does not promise equal outcomes. The base you start compounding from isn't always one you chose. Some people start with literacy in a house full of books. Some don't. Some start with savings and stable housing. Some don't. Some start with a network of people who can vouch for them. Some don't.

The principle still works. It just doesn't work *equally*. Pretending otherwise is the kind of advice that gets handed down by people who forgot what they started with. The next right thing is the only available mechanism for everyone — but the rate at which it compounds is not the same for everyone, and the truthful version of this argument has to say so.

What the principle does promise — and this is enough — is that wherever you currently stand, the next right thing leaves you better positioned than you were a moment ago. The math is honest. The compounding is real. The pace is yours. That is the only promise, and it is the one promise the song actually makes.

Anna in the cave didn't know she was loading the bases. She was just trying to do the one thing she could see how to do. That turned out to be the whole strategy.

You probably can't take the last step first.

You can almost always take the next one.
